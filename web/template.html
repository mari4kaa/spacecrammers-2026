    What PSI covers for your scenario

    Every PSI flight test ran in microgravity: aboard the ISS (in the Microgravity Science Glovebox, the Combustion Integrated Rack, or hand-held in the cabin) or, for Saffire, inside an uncrewed Cygnus after it left the ISS. Counts are table rows; one test can have several rows (SAME-R logs each smoke sample at several aging times).

      Atmospheres from Earth to spacesuit

      Spacesuits hold pure O₂ at only 4.3 psi so they stay flexible, and crews pre-breathe O₂ before a spacewalk to purge nitrogen and avoid the bends. A Moon base or pressurized rover at an in-between atmosphere (expected 30% O₂ or more at 10.2 psi or less) shortens that pre-breathe, but it has over 40% more O₂ by fraction than Earth air, which makes materials burn more easily. This chart shows where PSI tests sit on that path.

      Partial gravity: bounds and what is coming

      PSI has no partial-gravity combustion data yet; NASA expects LUCI and FM2 results to be added. Until then, microgravity and normal-gravity results can bound the Moon and Mars case, and ACME E-FIELD imitates buoyancy with an electric field.

          Fire-safety evidence from the experiments

          PreventionDo small flames keep burning in still air? SuppressionHow low must a suppressant push the oxygen? DetectionWill a smoke detector see it?

          Three questions a habitat designer asks, answered with measured flight data copied from the reports and papers behind the cards: will a small fire keep burning (prevention), how far must a suppressant cut the oxygen (suppression), and will the smoke be seen (detection). The data are fixed published measurements; your scenario from the bar above is compared against them (oxygen and pressure for prevention, oxygen for suppression), and each panel says when your conditions were never tested.

          Prevention: do small flames keep burning in still air?

          ACME BRE on the ISS: a 25 mm gas burner set up to burn like a solid material, with no air flow. 83 tests, each either burned until the fuel was shut off or put itself out. Oxygen and pressure were not varied independently: most 1-bar tests ran at 18-25% O₂.

          Suppression: how low must a suppressant push the oxygen?

          FLEX on the ISS: the lowest oxygen at which a burning fuel droplet can keep going (limiting oxygen index) at 1 atm, with the air diluted by nitrogen, carbon dioxide or helium. A suppressant works only if it brings the oxygen below the bar.

          Detection: will a smoke detector see it?

          SAME on the ISS: smoke from four spacecraft materials heated short of ignition, measured fresh and after aging. The lines mark the ISS smoke detector's stated limits: minimum reported sensitivity 0.3 µm, most sensitive above 1 µm.

          Unified test matrix

          19 PSI experiment tables cleaned into one schema: fuel, phase, size, O₂, pressure, air speed, diluent, outcome, a key measurement, plus where each value came from (o2_source, p_source) and quality flags. Every row keeps its PSI file and row number.

          CSV files hold data only. How they were made, including AI use (cleaning rules written with Claude), is described in data/README.md and data/datapackage.json in the repository; the JSON export carries the same note.

          Data-quality report

          Everything the cleaning step found and what it did. Fixes are applied in the unified matrix; items marked check are kept as-is and flagged for a human.

            How AI was used (Space Apps disclosure)

            InfoFlame was built by team Spacecrammers with AI assistance. The team chose the approach and features, supplied papers, reviewed the results, asked for corrections and published the site. The AI work is listed below; the same statement is in AI_USE.md in the repository, in the page metadata and in the script headers. The page contains no AI-generated images or video: the photos are NASA images credited under each one, and charts are drawn by Plotly from the data. It uses no NASA logos, flags or mission insignia and is not an official NASA product.
